## 1. Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, and a large fraction of life-threatening cardiac events first appears as transient rhythm or conduction abnormalities that escape a standard ten-second clinical ECG. Long-term ambulatory monitoring closes this detection gap, and deep neural networks have shown that arrhythmia patterns in ambulatory single-lead recordings can be recognized at cardiologist-level agreement when enough annotated data are available [#11]. The dominant ambulatory hardware, however, has changed far more slowly than the algorithms. Conventional Holter monitors rely on disposable Ag/AgCl gel electrodes whose electrolyte dries out within one to two days, frequently cause skin irritation during multi-day wear, and record raw signals that are interpreted offline, after the recording session ends [#9, #10]. Computer-interpreted ECG has well-documented benefits, but also well-documented limitations, which demand honest and limitation-aware evaluation whenever automated interpretation is deployed [#48]. A practical home monitoring device therefore needs three properties at once: electrodes that remain comfortable and electrically stable for days, local interpretation that does not depend on a network connection, and an evaluation protocol whose reported accuracy survives contact with real deployment conditions.

A second trend pushes interpretation from the cloud toward the microcontroller. Running inference on MCU-class hardware keeps raw biosignals on the device, removes round-trip latency, and cuts system power by roughly an order of magnitude relative to single-board computers or phone-offloaded pipelines [#2, #4]. TensorFlow Lite Micro and comparable runtimes now make INT8-quantized convolutional networks practical within a few hundred kilobytes of SRAM, and several groups have demonstrated ECG inference on Cortex-M, RISC-V, and ESP32-class chips [#1, #3, #6]. These systems remain algorithm-centric: most evaluate with intra-patient splits on a single public dataset, report binary normal-versus-abnormal detection only, and stop at the digital boundary, with no electrode, no analog front end, and no application layer. Quantization and preprocessing are typically treated as implementation details, although recent work shows that INT8 conversion alone can collapse multi-task ECG classifier performance [#7], and the mismatch between the offline zero-phase filtering used in training and the causal filters required at run time is, to our knowledge, not quantified anywhere in the MCU ECG literature.

A third trend comes from materials science, where conformal dry electrodes based on PEDOT:PSS blends and related biocomposites now reach skin-contact impedance and noise floors comparable to clinical gel electrodes, while adding self-adhesion, sweat tolerance, breathability, and stretchability [#29, #51, #57]. Epidermal electronics has matured from early demonstrations [#43] into a rich design space that has been reviewed comprehensively [#9], and prospective clinical comparisons confirm that modern dry electrodes acquire diagnostically acceptable ECG in practice [#10]. What this literature rarely provides is the rest of the system: electrode papers typically validate signal quality at the analog boundary and leave interpretation, wireless delivery, and evaluation methodology to others. Conversely, the best-known flexible ECG systems with integrated intelligence, such as the AI-enabled patch of Huang et al. [#5], achieve high integration with custom silicon but do not report patient-level multi-dataset evaluation, per-class AAMI breakdowns, or training-deployment consistency analysis, and their custom hardware remains out of reach for most teaching and low-resource laboratories.

Each ingredient of a trustworthy home ECG device therefore exists in isolation, but nowhere in combination. This paper presents a complete, low-cost flexible ECG system built around a commercial ESP32-S3 dual-core microcontroller, and makes four contributions. First, a dual-expert on-device AI: two INT8 1D-CNN experts, one trained for arrhythmia beat classification and one for myocardial infarction detection, are fused by a logical OR rule so that a single-lead wearable flags either abnormality class in real time; to our knowledge, this arrhythmia-plus-MI dual-expert fusion has not been reported on MCU-class hardware. Second, deployment-chain-matched training: we quantify the accuracy cost of training on zero-phase (filtfilt) preprocessed data while deploying behind the firmware's causal filter chain (up to ΔAUC −0.105 on the PTB domain) and recover most of that cost by retraining on data preprocessed through an exact software replica of the deployed filter chain. Third, evaluation rigor: all headline results use patient-level 60/20/20 splits across MIT-BIH, INCART, and PTB with an explicit leakage audit, and we report per-class behavior under the AAMI grouping, which exposes per-class ceilings that aggregate accuracy hides. Fourth, a complete and reproducible stack: a self-made PEDOT:PSS:PVA:TA flexible dry electrode (reproducing the formulation of Cao et al. [#29]), an AD8232 analog front end, dual-core firmware combining digital filtering and Pan-Tompkins heart-rate detection [#31] with on-device inference, and a BLE link to a Flutter smartphone application, all assembled from commercial off-the-shelf parts at a cost of a few tens of US dollars.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work across ten themes and positions this system against its closest competitors. Section 3 describes the system design: electrode, analog front end, firmware, on-device AI, and the BLE application layer. Section 4 details the datasets, the patient-level splitting and leakage audit, training and deployment-chain matching, the evaluation protocol, the human-subject validation protocol, and the on-device benchmarking protocol. Sections 5-7 present results, discussion, and conclusions. The system described here is a research prototype intended for methodology and engineering evaluation; it is not a medical device and is not intended for clinical diagnosis.

## 2. Related Work

This section moves from algorithms to hardware to systems, then to methodology (evaluation rigor, training-deployment consistency, and model fusion), and closes with a head-to-head comparison against the closest high-profile competitor and an explicit gap statement. The ordering is deliberate: each pillar of the present work exists somewhere in the literature in isolation, and the review builds toward the observation that no published work combines all four.

### 2.1. On-Device ECG AI on Microcontroller-Class Hardware

A growing line of work runs ECG inference directly on MCU-class hardware, spanning general-purpose Cortex-M and ESP32 chips as well as specialized ultra-low-power SoCs, but these efforts stay algorithm-centric and stop short of full system integration or evaluation rigor. ArrythML targets the same ESP32-S3 as our system, using an INT8 autoencoder for binary normal/abnormal detection [#1] (preprint, not peer-reviewed). Busi et al. deploy a tiny Transformer on the GAP9 RISC-V SoC [#3], and Zhou et al. implement an LSTM classifier on a Cortex-M4 together with a complete PCB design [#6]. Farag et al. report an inter-patient CNN evaluated across three public databases, but on a Raspberry Pi rather than on bare-metal MCU hardware [#2]. Rajotte et al. supply an important caution: INT8 quantization can collapse multi-task ECG classification performance [#7].

Relative to these works, the differences are as follows. ArrythML [#1] shares our exact MCU but offers binary detection only, with intra-patient evaluation and no analog front end, electrode, BLE link, or application. Busi [#3] and Zhou [#6] rely on specialized or different silicon and evaluate intra-patient on a single dataset. Farag [#2] is inter-patient but runs on a Linux single-board computer, not a microcontroller. The INT8 risk quantified by Rajotte [#7] is one we address directly, by measuring both quantization and filter-chain effects and by matching training to the deployment pipeline (Section 4.3). None of these works fuses two diagnostic experts, audits patient leakage, or ships a complete acquisition-to-application stack.

### 2.2. Deep Learning Models for ECG Classification (Cloud and Benchmark Scale)

Deep networks have reached cardiologist-level performance on large ECG corpora. Hannun et al. demonstrated cardiologist-level arrhythmia detection and classification in ambulatory recordings [#11]; Ribeiro et al. extended deep diagnosis to the full 12-lead ECG [#27]; Acharya et al. established the deep CNN heartbeat classifier [#26]; and Kachuee et al. showed that learned beat representations transfer across datasets and tasks [#44]. Hybrid Transformer architectures continue to push benchmark accuracy [#13], and plain 1D-CNN arrhythmia classifiers remain strong, simple baselines [#14]. The architectural priors behind most modern ECG networks, namely residual learning [#34] and attention [#35], come from the wider deep learning literature.

These works define the accuracy ceiling and supply the one-dimensional convolutional and residual building blocks that our approximately 80K-parameter ResNet-L INT8 model adapts to a 32 KB tensor arena. They do not address MCU memory and latency budgets, on-device INT8 quantization, dry-electrode signal quality, or deployment-chain fidelity. We therefore treat them as method foundations rather than as system competitors.

### 2.3. Flexible Dry Electrodes for Biopotential Acquisition

Materials research has produced a rich family of conformal, sweat-tolerant dry electrodes, including the PEDOT:PSS blend class that our electrode reproduces, but this literature evaluates electrode-level signal quality and stops at the analog boundary. Cao et al. introduced the stretchable, self-adhesive PEDOT:PSS:PVA:TA blend with high sweat tolerance whose formulation our electrode follows [#29]. Closely related systems include fully organic compliant self-adhesive dry electrodes [#57], adhesive biocomposite electrodes for sweaty skin [#51], breathable fabric-based hydrogel electrodes [#49], air-permeable waterproof ECG patches [#52], screen-printed gentle-to-skin wet electrodes with compact wireless readout [#53], and silk-based sweat-tolerant on-skin electrodes [#54]. The field descends from epidermal electronics [#43] and stretchable epidermal ECG systems [#42], and it has been reviewed comprehensively elsewhere [#9]. On the validation side, Vidhya et al. compared dry and gel electrodes prospectively in 50 subjects [#10], and Bosnjak et al. established skin-electrode impedance spectroscopy practice for dry electrodes [#55].

This literature optimizes impedance, adhesion, breathability, and motion tolerance, and it validates ECG waveforms against gel references [#10, #55]. We reproduce and characterize a known formulation [#29] rather than claiming materials novelty; our contribution begins where these papers end, namely in connecting the electrode to an analog front end, an on-device dual-expert AI, and a phone application within one measured chain.

### 2.4. Electrode-to-System Integration (AFE + MCU + AI Stacks)

A smaller set of works integrates flexible electrodes with readout electronics into complete monitoring systems, including 12-lead flexible arrays from our advisors' groups, but on-device AI with rigorous evaluation is usually absent. Wan et al. demonstrated a flexible and stretchable 12-lead ECG system with individually deformable interconnects [#30], and Zhang et al. presented activatable conformal electrode arrays for highly stable and breathable 12-lead clinical monitoring [#46]. Xu et al. integrated dry electrodes with silicon in an ECG-on-chip approach [#8]. Wireless wearable sensors for long-term ambulatory monitoring were established earlier by Nemati et al. [#40], and the architecture-level challenges of wearable ECG systems are surveyed by Li et al. [#41]. Closest to our hardware budget, Panwar et al. built AD8232-plus-Arduino acquisition with PC-offloaded inference [#4], and Xie et al. reported wearable multilead ECG sensing with stretchable, breathable dry adhesives [#56].

The differences are again complementary. Wan [#30] and Zhang [#46] deliver high-channel-count flexible hardware but no embedded inference. Xu [#8] targets chip-level sensing rather than a trainable diagnostic model. Panwar [#4] shares our AFE chip and our undergraduate-first-author profile, yet offloads inference to a PC and uses an 8-bit, 16 MHz MCU with disposable Ag/AgCl electrodes. Our system keeps the full chain on one low-cost board: a flexible dry electrode, an AD8232 front end, a dual-core ESP32-S3 running digital filtering, Pan-Tompkins heart-rate detection, and INT8 inference on separate cores, and BLE delivery to a Flutter application.

### 2.5. Patient-Level and Cross-Domain Evaluation Methodology

Benchmark datasets and inter-patient protocols have exposed how intra-patient splits inflate ECG classifier performance, yet most MCU-scale papers still report intra-patient or single-dataset numbers. Strodthoff et al. provided deep learning benchmarks and evaluation insights on PTB-XL [#15], and PTB-XL itself has become a standard large-scale resource [#24]. PhysioNet hosts the reference databases used throughout this field [#23], and the PhysioNet/CinC Challenge 2017 defined short single-lead atrial fibrillation classification as a community benchmark [#25]. Hasani et al. addressed multi-source domain generalization on 12-lead ECG with adversarial training [#19]. Farag et al. applied an inter-patient protocol across MIT-BIH, INCART, and PTB, the same three databases we use [#2].

Farag's work [#2] comes closest methodologically, but it reports aggregate metrics without an AAMI class-wise breakdown and without an explicit leakage audit. We adopt patient-level splits across all three databases, audit the splits for patient leakage, and report per-class behavior under the AAMI grouping, including the fusion beat ceiling (recall 0.442) that aggregate accuracy hides. Hasani's domain generalization results [#19] motivate our cross-domain concern, which we operationalize at MCU scale with deployment-chain-matched transfer evaluation (Section 4.4).

### 2.6. Training-Deployment Consistency (Filter Chain Mismatch)

ECG preprocessing is usually treated as an implementation detail, yet the choice between offline zero-phase filtering and real-time causal filtering changes the signal the deployed model actually sees, and this mismatch is rarely quantified. Gregg et al. engineered an efficient linear-phase high-pass filter for ECG [#18]. Rajotte et al. quantified how INT8 quantization can collapse multi-task ECG classification performance [#7]. The algorithm of Pan and Tompkins remains the reference for causal, real-time, QRS-oriented filtering [#31]. These works improve filters or quantify quantization harm, but none of them measures the train-versus-deploy filter mismatch itself.

We quantify this mismatch directly: training on filtfilt-preprocessed data while deploying behind the firmware's causal biquad and comb chain costs up to ΔAUC −0.105 on the PTB domain (Section 4.4), and we remove about half of that cost with deployment-chain-matched training, in which the preprocessing applied to the training data mirrors the firmware filter exactly. Related analyses of filter-induced ST distortion exist outside the MCU inference setting (e.g., linear-phase high-pass designs that eliminate causal-IIR ST overshoot [#18]); our contribution is scoped to the specific, previously unquantified setting of on-device ECG inference on MCU-class hardware, where train-versus-deploy filter mismatch is measured end-to-end on the deployed chain. To our knowledge, this end-to-end quantification has not previously been reported for MCU ECG inference.

### 2.7. Multi-Expert and Hybrid Models for ECG Interpretation

Hybrid and multi-branch architectures have been explored to combine complementary feature views of the ECG, but fusion across distinct diagnostic tasks, specifically arrhythmia beat classification and myocardial infarction (MI) detection, remains unexplored on edge hardware. Alghieth's DeepECG-Net combines Transformer and convolutional branches for real-time anomaly detection [#13]. Makimoto et al. showed that a CNN can recognize myocardial infarction from ECG [#17], and Al-Zaiti et al. demonstrated machine learning for occlusion MI diagnosis and risk stratification at clinical scale [#36]. Kachuee et al. showed that deep representations transfer across ECG tasks [#44], and Al Rahhal et al. explored deep active classification of electrocardiogram signals [#47].

Prior work treats arrhythmia classification [#11, #26] and MI detection [#17, #36] as separate problems, usually on separate datasets and platforms. We train two INT8 experts, one for arrhythmia beat classification and one for MI detection, and fuse them with an OR rule, so that a single-lead wearable flags either abnormality class in real time. This dual-expert OR fusion for arrhythmia plus MI has not been reported on MCU-class hardware.

### 2.8. Real-Time Heart-Rate Detection on Resource-Constrained Hardware

The Pan-Tompkins algorithm remains the reference causal QRS detector for embedded ECG [#31], but published MCU systems rarely validate their heart-rate chain against an annotated public database with standard sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), and F1 reporting. Computer-interpreted ECG more broadly carries documented benefits and limitations that motivate conservative and transparent reporting of automated measurements [#48]. Long-term ambulatory monitoring systems such as that of Nemati et al. [#40] provide the application context in which embedded heart-rate estimation must remain trustworthy over days of wear.

We implement an energy-envelope QRS detector in the Pan-Tompkins family on the acquisition core and validate it against the annotated LUDB database, reporting sensitivity of 96.40%, PPV of 78.87%, F1 of 0.868, and a BPM error of 1.46 median / 4.16 mean (Section 5.4), rather than reporting only in-system plausibility. Schläpfer and Wellens [#48] articulate why honest, limitation-aware reporting of automated ECG measurements matters; our AAMI class-wise and LUDB reporting follows that guidance.

### 2.9. Wearable ECG Mobile Ecosystems (BLE + Smartphone Apps)

Wireless wearable ECG systems commonly stream to phones for display, but the phone is usually a passive viewer while interpretation stays off-device or is absent entirely. Nemati et al. demonstrated a wireless wearable ECG sensor for long-term ambulatory monitoring [#40]; Li et al. surveyed the architecture, challenges, and future directions of wearable ECG monitoring systems [#41]; Panwar et al. paired an AD8232 with an HC-05 Bluetooth module and external compute [#4]; and Rauf et al. built a compact wireless readout for screen-printed electrodes [#53]. In these systems, the wireless link carries raw or lightly processed signal and the application merely displays it.

Our BLE Nordic UART Service link instead carries the on-device AI output alongside the waveform, with an abnormal flag and a confidence value embedded in every data frame, so the Flutter application renders on-device inference in real time. Li et al. [#41] catalog this exact gap between sensing hardware and closed-loop interpretation; our stack closes it at undergraduate-budget cost.

### 2.10. Competitor Benchmarking: Positioning Against Huang 2025

The closest high-profile system is the AI-enabled flexible ECG patch of Huang et al., published in *National Science Review* [#5], and an honest head-to-head comparison shows that our work occupies a different, complementary niche rather than competing on integration density. Huang et al. deliver custom, highly integrated AI hardware at the patch level, with higher channel integration and a higher-tier venue. We do not claim to match that integration density or clinical validation depth.

Where the present work differs and contributes is fivefold: (1) a commercial off-the-shelf ESP32-S3 (roughly 0.5 W active, costing a few US dollars) instead of custom silicon, making the full stack reproducible by undergraduates and low-resource laboratories; (2) dual-expert arrhythmia-plus-MI OR fusion, which [#5] does not report; (3) deployment-chain-matched training with a quantified causal-versus-filtfilt mismatch (ΔAUC up to −0.105), which [#5] does not report; (4) patient-level multi-dataset evaluation with an AAMI class-wise breakdown and an explicit leakage audit, which [#5] does not report; and (5) a fully documented open toolchain from electrode recipe to Flutter application. Against the remaining direct competitors, ArrythML [#1] shares our MCU but is binary-only and system-incomplete; Farag et al. [#2] are inter-patient but not MCU-deployed; Busi et al. [#3] are on-device but intra-patient and electrode-free; and Panwar et al. [#4] share our AFE but offload inference. The present system is the only entry that is simultaneously full-stack, on-device, patient-level, and deployment-chain-matched.

### 2.11. Gap Statement

Individually, each ingredient of this work exists in the literature: on-device ECG inference on MCU-class hardware [#1, #3, #6], flexible PEDOT:PSS-class dry electrodes [#29, #57], inter-patient multi-dataset evaluation [#2], and flexible-electrode systems with integrated AI [#5]. No single published work, however, combines all four of the following: (a) dual-expert OR fusion of arrhythmia beat classification and myocardial infarction detection on device; (b) deployment-chain-matched training in which the mismatch between offline zero-phase (filtfilt) preprocessing and the deployed causal filter chain is quantified (here, up to ΔAUC −0.105 on the PTB domain) and corrected; (c) patient-level multi-dataset evaluation with an AAMI class-wise breakdown that exposes per-class ceilings such as a fusion beat recall of 0.442; and (d) a complete, low-cost stack from flexible dry electrode through analog front end, dual-core MCU inference, and BLE to a smartphone application. The closest high-profile system, Huang et al. [#5], delivers (d) with custom patch-level silicon but reports neither (a), (b), nor (c), while the closest MCU works [#1, #3] report neither (b), (c), nor a complete electrode-to-application chain. This paper fills that intersection with a reproducible ESP32-S3 platform and an evaluation protocol designed so that its reported numbers survive deployment.

## 3. System Design

The system acquires a single-lead ECG through a self-made flexible dry electrode, conditions the signal with an AD8232 analog front end, and processes it on an ESP32-S3 dual-core microcontroller running at 240 MHz. Core 1 handles 500 Hz sampling, digital filtering, Pan-Tompkins heart-rate detection, and communication; Core 0 runs INT8 deep inference on 250-sample windows through TensorFlow Lite Micro. Results stream over USB serial and a BLE Nordic UART Service link to a PC plotting tool and a Flutter smartphone application. A software signal generator (five-Gaussian P-QRS-T synthesis with IEC 60601-2-51 scenario-3 noise) provides a bench-test mode that exercises the identical digital chain without a human subject. ### 3.1. Flexible Dry Electrode

The electrode reproduces the stretchable, self-adhesive PEDOT:PSS blend formulation of Cao et al. [#29]: PEDOT:PSS, poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), and tannic acid (TA) are solution-blended at a weight ratio of 20:40:40, doped with 5 wt% ethylene glycol (EG), cast, and dried into free-standing films of 20-25 µm thickness. Films are cut into 25 × 25 mm² electrodes for ECG acquisition (30 × 10 mm² strips for peel testing). PVA provides the stretchable matrix, TA provides wet-stable adhesion through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, and EG raises the conductivity of the PEDOT:PSS phase. We claim no materials novelty; the contribution of this work is the integration and characterization of this known formulation within a complete acquisition-to-inference chain.

Table T2 lists the literature reference values for this formulation against which our in-house characterization (protocol in Section 4.5) is benchmarked: conductivity of 122 S/cm, elongation at break of 54%, Young's modulus of 18.3 MPa, 90° peel adhesion of 0.32 N/cm on dry and 0.28 N/cm on wet skin, skin-contact impedance at 10 Hz of 256 kΩ·cm² dry and 100 kΩ·cm² wet (versus 410 kΩ·cm² for Ag/AgCl), resting ECG RMS noise of 11.8 µV dry and 12.2 µV wet (versus 12.6 µV for Ag/AgCl), and relative resistance change R/R0 of 1.15 at 30% and 1.36 at 51% tensile strain [#29]. ### 3.2. Analog Front End

The analog front end (AFE) is built around the AD8232 single-lead heart-rate monitor chip, which integrates an instrumentation amplifier, operational amplifiers for mid-band gain and filtering, and a right-leg-drive (RLD) amplifier, and operates from a single 3.3 V supply. The system uses the standard three-electrode configuration (right arm, left arm, right leg drive), with common-mode rejection provided by the instrumentation amplifier stage (CMRR ≥ 86 dB, per the AD8232 data sheet). The AD8232 output feeds an ADC channel of the ESP32-S3 (GPIO4) on a custom two-layer PCB that mates the AFE with the ESP32-S3-SUPERMINI module. The same AD8232 chip serves as the front end in the comparable low-cost system of Panwar et al. [#4], which makes it a useful reference point for reproducibility.

### 3.3. Firmware and Real-Time Signal Chain

The firmware samples the AFE output at 500 Hz (2 ms per sample) and produces three synchronized channels per sample: the raw (de-biased) signal, the noise-injected signal in simulator mode, and the digitally filtered signal. Serial output is decimated to 25 Hz for logging; the full-rate stream feeds the on-device processing chain.

Digital filtering uses three cascaded second-order IIR biquad sections in transposed direct form II: a second-order Butterworth high-pass at 0.05 Hz for baseline-wander removal (lowered from 0.5 Hz to the AHA 2007 diagnostic corner to limit causal-phase ST-segment distortion; the residual +8.1° phase error at 0.5 Hz versus +90° at the old corner is analyzed in Section 4.4), a second-order Butterworth low-pass at 40 Hz for myoelectric-noise suppression, and a second-order 50 Hz notch with Q = 20 for power-line interference. All coefficients are designed offline (MATLAB) and hard-coded, avoiding run-time coefficient computation. In front of the biquad chain, a dual-stage comb filter exploits the exact 500/50 sampling ratio: two cascaded 10-tap moving averages provide −59.6 dB attenuation at 50 Hz per stage (−119.2 dB total), additionally suppress the 100 Hz harmonic, and introduce a group delay of 20 ms, well below a typical RR interval of about 800 ms. This causal, fixed-point-friendly chain is the deployment-time counterpart of the offline zero-phase preprocessing discussed in Sections 2.6 and 4.3; the causal reference practice for real-time ECG filtering dates to Pan and Tompkins [#31], and linear-phase alternatives such as that of Gregg et al. [#18] remain offline-only options.

Heart-rate estimation uses an energy-envelope QRS detector in the Pan-Tompkins family [#31] on the filtered channel: an 8-25 Hz bandpass emphasizes the QRS complex while suppressing T-waves, the squared signal (energy envelope, without the Pan-Tompkins derivative) is integrated over an 80 ms moving window, and local maxima above an adaptive threshold (a noise-peak estimate plus 0.30 times the difference between the signal-peak and noise-peak estimates) are accepted under a 200 ms refractory period; RR intervals are timed in wall-clock milliseconds and their running median is converted to the BPM output. Detections in the first 520 ms are blanked while the filters settle, and from the third accepted beat onward three energy-domain morphology gates re-validate each candidate against the LUDB-recalibrated operating point: rise/fall asymmetry ratio ≤ 40 (T-waves reach ≈ 65-70), peak amplitude ≥ 0.55× the previous valid peak for short RR intervals (< 0.9 s), and RR ≥ 0.65× the running median RR. Each output frame also carries a signal-quality index (SQI, 0-1) and a motion flag derived from the signal statistics.

Computation is split across the two cores under FreeRTOS. Core 1 runs the acquisition loop: sampling, comb and biquad filtering, heart-rate detection, 2:1 decimation (500 Hz to 250 Hz) into a 250-sample inference ring buffer, and communication. Every 125 decimated samples (0.5 s), Core 1 releases a binary semaphore; Core 0 waits on the semaphore, copies the window under mutex protection, runs inference (Section 3.4), and posts results to a queue of depth 8 that Core 1 drains non-blocking into the output frames. The main acquisition buffer holds 1500 samples (3 s at 500 Hz). A thermal supervisor samples the on-chip temperature sensor once per second (eight-point moving average) and throttles the CPU from 240 MHz to 60 MHz above 65 °C, restoring 240 MHz below 55 °C.

On-device inference runs on TensorFlow Lite Micro with full-integer INT8 quantization. Each expert is a one-dimensional residual CNN of the ResNet-L class (approximately 80K parameters), following the residual-block design of He et al. [#34] and the transferable-representation practice of Kachuee et al. [#44]. The inference window is 250 samples, exactly 1.0 s at the decimated 250 Hz rate, matching the training window; the stride is 250 samples (1.0 s) — the earlier 0.5 s stride was abandoned after the measured single-inference latency (~910 ms) exceeded it and starved the inference core into watchdog resets. Per window, the engine applies Z-score normalization, quantizes to INT8 (round-to-nearest with the model's scale and zero-point), invokes the interpreter, dequantizes the abnormal-class probability directly without a second softmax (a double softmax compresses the probability dynamic range to [0.27, 0.73] and silently shifts every threshold's semantics), and raises the abnormal flag when P(abnormal) exceeds the decision threshold. The shipped firmware runs θ = 0.60 with five-beat confirmation — a conservative operating point adopted after real-AFE domain shift was observed (Section 5.2); the evaluation operating points (beat θ = 0.35, patient θ = 0.5) are studied in Section 4.4 and must not be quoted as firmware settings. The tensor arena occupies 64 KB of SRAM (allocation measured at 40,004 B), and the interpreter runs as a FreeRTOS task pinned to Core 0, fully decoupled from acquisition on Core 1 through the semaphore/ring-buffer mechanism of Section 3.3.

Two experts cover two diagnostic tasks: an arrhythmia expert trained on merged MIT-BIH and INCART beat data, and a myocardial infarction expert trained on PTB. Their binary decisions are fused by a logical OR rule, so a window is flagged abnormal when either expert fires, and every output frame carries the fused abnormal flag together with a confidence value. The intent is that a single-lead wearable can flag either abnormality class in real time without doubling the sensing hardware. Two implementation notes keep this claim honest. First, the INT8 failure mode documented by Rajotte et al. [#7] is a real risk for small multi-task models, which is one reason we deploy two single-task experts rather than one multi-task network. Second, the current on-board model is the real-data domain-adapted expert, **exp7c** (Section 5.2): the deployment-chain-matched exp6-SGD was integrated first (2026-08-05, replacing the legacy CNN-v2), and after real-AFE confidence shift was measured, the fine-tuned exp7c INT8 model (167,376 B) replaced it in the firmware model header (`ecg_model_data.h`, 2026-08-14) and passed on-device verification. The dual-interpreter configuration (two interpreters, 2 × 64 KB arenas) fits the ESP32-S3 SRAM budget by construction; its Flash budget is a non-issue on the current 16 MB module (2% for two models). Measured on-device latency, memory, and power are [待补充: pending execution of the on-device benchmark protocol, Section 4.6].

### 3.5. BLE Link and Application Layer

The system communicates over Bluetooth Low Energy using the Nordic UART Service (NUS): service UUID 6E400001-B5A3-F393-E0A9-E50E24DCCA9E, TX (notify) characteristic 6E400002-B5A3-F393-E0A9-E50E24DCCA9E, and RX (write) characteristic 6E400003-B5A3-F393-E0A9-E50E24DCCA9E. The radio transmits at +9 dBm, and frames are batched four at a time to reduce notify overhead. Every frame is a nine-column CSV record carrying the full system state: clean, noisy, and filtered signal channels; the on-board BPM estimate; a true-BPM field used only in simulator mode; the SQI; the motion flag; the AI abnormal flag; and the AI confidence. Because the AI output travels inside the same stream as the waveform, any receiver (phone app or PC tool) renders interpretation and signal together with no additional protocol. This differs from the raw-signal streaming of prior wearable systems [#4, #40] and follows the closed-loop direction identified by Li et al. [#41].

The smartphone application (Flutter, with flutter_blue_plus and provider) scans for and connects to the "ESP32-ECG" device, renders the three channels in real time with a custom painter, displays BPM and signal quality, highlights AI-flagged abnormal windows, and provides sweep-speed and amplitude controls (1/2/4/6 s windows). On the PC side, `ecg_plotter.py` provides equivalent real-time plotting plus CSV logging over the serial link, and serves as the recording front end for the human-subject protocol (Section 4.5). The BLE protocol stack is the dominant on-board heat source, which motivates the thermal supervision described in Section 3.3.

## 4. Materials and Methods

This section specifies the datasets and preprocessing, the patient-level splitting and leakage audit, model training including deployment-chain matching, the evaluation protocol, the human-subject validation protocol, and the on-device benchmarking protocol. All quantitative values in this section are drawn from the project's authoritative results ledger (`docs/FINAL_RESULTS.md`, generated from `patient_split_eval.json` and `deploy_match/retrain_exp6_sgd_eval.json`); values that do not yet exist in a named source are marked [待补充: ...] rather than estimated.

### 4.1. Datasets and Preprocessing

Three public databases, all distributed through PhysioNet [#23], are used (Table T3). The MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database contributes 48 half-hour two-lead records from 47 patients (records 201 and 202 come from the same subject), yielding 658,962 annotated beats after repair of the record set, of which 543,786 are normal and 115,176 abnormal. The St. Petersburg INCART 12-lead Arrhythmia Database contributes 75 records from 32 patients; because several patients contribute multiple records, record-level splitting of INCART leaks badly and only patient-level splitting is meaningful (Section 4.2). MIT-BIH and INCART are merged into one arrhythmia corpus of 123 records from 79 patients (834,741 beats; 834,495 is the sequence count of the historical three-beat evaluation track, not the beat count) for the arrhythmia expert. The PTB Diagnostic ECG Database serves the myocardial infarction (MI) task, following the PTB-based MI recognition practice of Makimoto et al. [#17]: of the original 549 records, 16 were excluded because signal quality prevented QRS detection (zero detected peaks), leaving 533 records from 290 patients with 69,482 extracted beats (10,416 normal, 59,066 abnormal).

All signals are resampled to 250 Hz and segmented into beat-centric windows of 250 samples (1.0 s), matching the on-device inference window. Training data for the arrhythmia task receive 6× augmentation (time shifts, amplitude scaling, and additive noise); PTB is used without augmentation. Beat-level classification into normal and abnormal classes follows established CNN heartbeat-classification practice [#26]. Two input granularities exist in the legacy experiments (single-beat 250-sample windows and three-beat 750-sample sequences); their PTB test sets differ in patient composition because sequence stitching drops some records (13,058 versus 13,322 test beats), so beat counts must not be compared across input sizes. Main results in this paper use the 250-sample single-beat granularity that matches the deployed system.

### 4.2. Patient-Level Splitting and Leakage Audit

All headline evaluations use patient-level 60/20/20 train/validation/test splits with seed 42 and permutation semantics, following inter-patient evaluation practice [#2, #15]. The merged MIT+INCART corpus is split into 49 training, 15 validation, and 15 test patients; PTB is split into 172 training, 57 validation, and 57 test patients. The intersection of training and test patient sets was computed explicitly and verified to be empty for every reported split.

Because earlier project experiments used record-level or beat-level random splits, a formal leakage audit was conducted. It found two mechanisms: (1) under record-level splitting with seed-42 `choice` semantics, approximately 17% of PTB test beats originated from patients also present in training, so the model had effectively seen the test patients; and (2) INCART's multi-record patients were dispersed across train and test partitions, causing severe record-level leakage. Metrics produced under those historical splits (for example, PTB AUC values up to 0.9939) are retained in this paper only inside a dedicated methodological-contrast subsection of the Results, where they quantify how much intra-patient leakage inflates performance; they are never reported as headline results. This contrast is the empirical counterpart of the inter-patient concerns raised by Strodthoff et al. [#15] and Farag et al. [#2].

One limitation is reported transparently: the MIT/INCART test sets contain 6× augmented beats that are highly correlated with their original beats, so the effective number of independent test samples is overestimated for that corpus. PTB test beats are unaugmented and unaffected by this limitation.

### 4.3. Model Training and Deployment-Chain Matching

Both experts use the ResNet-L one-dimensional residual architecture (approximately 80K parameters) [#34, #44]. Training minimizes focal loss (γ = 1.0, α = 0.75) to counter the strong class imbalance, with label smoothing, mixup, and ECG-specific augmentation available in the training tooling. Patient-clean training semantics are enforced throughout: for the PTB domain, training beats are drawn only from the 172 training patients, and no test-patient data enters optimization. The three patient-clean experiment arms (exp4, exp5, exp6) differ in their domain composition and balancing strategy and are compared in the Results.

The deployment chain of the firmware (causal biquad high-pass 0.5 Hz, low-pass 40 Hz, 50 Hz notch, dual comb filter, and 2:1 decimation to 250 Hz) differs systematically from the original training chain (zero-phase `filtfilt` filtering plus FFT resampling). We quantify this mismatch by evaluating a `filtfilt`-trained model through the deployment chain: on the PTB domain, AUC drops from 0.8232 under the matched training chain (D0) to 0.7184 under the deployment chain (D3), a cost of ΔAUC −0.105. To correct it, training data are preprocessed through an exact software replica of the firmware chain, producing deployment-chain-matched (D3) training. For this retraining, stochastic gradient descent (learning rate 0.01, Nesterov momentum 0.9, weight decay 1×10⁻⁴, early-stopping patience 40, at most 50 epochs) outperforms the AdamW arm by +0.035 AUC on the PTB deployment-chain evaluation and is therefore the reported configuration. Trained FP32 models are converted to INT8 with the TFLite converter and exported as C arrays for firmware integration; PC-side INT8-versus-FP32 agreement is checked before export (Section 4.6), in view of the quantization risks documented by Rajotte et al. [#7].

Two further deployment-chain fidelity issues complete the picture. First, the acquisition loop initially had no hardware timer and ran as fast as its per-iteration workload allowed, so its actual rate drifted off the designed 500 Hz (about 336 Hz in AFE mode, about 516 Hz under the lighter simulator workload). Because the dual-stage 50 Hz comb filter is designed for the exact 500/50 = 10 sample ratio, a sub-500 Hz loop shifts the comb notch away from the power-line frequency, and the 2:1-decimated recording falls to an effective ~225.7 Hz rather than the nominal 250 Hz. Heart-rate RR timing was made frame-rate-independent (wall-clock milliseconds) to remove the resulting BPM overestimate. The sampling cadence is now enforced by an esp_timer hardware tick (2 ms period, 500 Hz) and was verified on the real AFE path in a 60 s serial capture: the main loop sustained 496.2 Hz (the residual 0.8% reflects two mode-switch transients and 1-2 tick backlogs per reporting period during steady state), and a 60 s recording stored 15,684 samples in 64 s, i.e., 245.1 Hz instead of the pre-fix 225.7 Hz. The comb notch therefore sits at 49.6 Hz, within 0.8% of the 50 Hz design point. This timing mismatch was a sample-rate-level deployment-chain gap distinct from, and additional to, the filter-chain mismatch quantified above.

Second, the deployment chain includes a real-data domain-adaptation step (exp7c). After the exp6-SGD model was flashed, real resting recordings from the device's own electrode and AFE exposed a confidence-shift failure mode that the database benchmarks do not capture: normal beats from the device domain scored at a mean abnormal confidence of 0.732 (81.4% above the 0.5 threshold). To correct this without regressing the database benchmarks, the frozen backbone was fine-tuned — only the fc1/out heads, Adam with learning rate 1e-5, sparse cross-entropy with class weight {0:4, 1:1} — on 210 real normal beats (180 training / 30 held out) plus 2,000 original abnormal beats (anti-forgetting) and 600 original normal beats, and the resulting exp7c model was exported to INT8 and flashed (Section 5.2).

### 4.4. Evaluation Protocol

The headline evaluation is patient-level: all test patients are disjoint from training patients (Section 4.2), windows are 250 samples, and the default decision threshold is θ = 0.5. Metrics are AUC, recall, precision, and F1, reported separately for the MIT-BIH/INCART domain and the PTB domain, because the two domains play different roles: exp4/exp5/exp6 saw PTB training patients, so their PTB results are in-domain, whereas their MIT-BIH/INCART results and all legacy cross-domain models measure transfer. Legacy cross-domain models were trained on the pre-repair 37-record MIT npz corpus without augmentation; because this training domain no longer matches the repaired 48-record 6× augmented corpus, their MIT-domain scores carry the "transfer" role as legacy-data-domain lower bounds, not as like-for-like comparisons.

Threshold behavior is characterized for the deployment candidates at θ ∈ {0.35, 0.5, 0.65, 0.8}; the θ = 0.35 operating point is reported as a sensitivity-oriented reference for screening use, while θ = 0.5 remains the headline point. Deployment-chain (D3) results are reported separately from training-chain (D0) results, and the two are never compared directly as if they shared a chain.

Per-class behavior is reported under the AAMI EC57 superclass grouping (N, SVEB, VEB, F, Q), in the spirit of limitation-aware reporting advocated by Schläpfer and Wellens [#48]. Aggregate AUC hides per-class ceilings: as an example, the fusion beat recall ceiling is 0.442 under our patient-level protocol, and per-class tables in the Results give every superclass the same prominence as the aggregate metrics. Uncertainty is quantified with patient-level bootstrap 95% confidence intervals [待补充: bootstrap resample count and CI values, to be inserted from the final evaluation run by todo 14].

### 4.5. Human-Subject Validation Protocol

The human-subject study is a device prototype validation, not a clinical trial, and the device is a research prototype that is not intended for clinical diagnosis. The protocol compares the self-made PEDOT:PSS dry electrode against a commercial Ag/AgCl gel electrode in a within-subject sequential design: each subject wears the Ag/AgCl electrodes first (as the reference standard) and the PEDOT:PSS electrodes second, at identical anatomical sites in a lead-II-like configuration (right arm and left arm below the clavicles, left leg at the lower rib margin). The sequential design is required because the AD8232 is a single-channel front end. Sample size is n = 5-10 healthy adult volunteers (≥ 18 years), consistent with prototype-validation practice in the flexible-electrode literature and far below the scale of clinical comparison studies such as Vidhya et al. (n = 50) [#10]. Inclusion requires self-reported good health without known cardiovascular disease; exclusion criteria are any cardiac history, implanted electronic devices, known skin allergy to adhesives, gels, or conductive polymers, heart-rate-affecting medication, pregnancy, and damaged skin at the test sites.

Each subject completes six action segments per electrode (Table T6): 5 min seated rest, 30 s arm swinging, 60 s recovery, 60 s marching in place, 60 s recovery, and 30 s paced deep breathing, yielding twelve CSV files per subject in the nine-column format of Section 3.5. Skin is cleaned with 75% alcohol before each electrode application, and real-time quality control (visual waveform inspection plus the on-board SQI) pauses recording if contact is lost. The analysis plan covers per-segment SNR (QRS peak against baseline RMS noise), SQI comparison between electrodes, heart-rate agreement (Bland-Altman analysis and Pearson correlation), the resting false-positive rate of the on-device abnormal flag, and motion robustness as the SQI drop from rest to motion segments; dry-versus-gel comparison and impedance practice follow Vidhya et al. [#10] and Bosnjak et al. [#55]. Data are anonymized (subject codes S01-S10; only sex and age are recorded).

Safety and ethics: the system is USB-powered (5 V, ≤ 500 mA), and subject isolation is provided by the AD8232 instrumentation amplifier (single 3.3 V supply, CMRR ≥ 86 dB). Electrode constituent materials (PEDOT:PSS, PVA, TA) are commonly reported as biocompatible in the epidermal-electrode literature [#29], but transient mild skin irritation cannot be excluded and is covered by the stop rules. **Ethics approval is pending at the time of writing**: the study will be executed only after the Tianjin University simplified ethics review path (or advisor-filed internal record, as confirmed with the supervisory team) has been completed and every subject has signed the informed consent form; the approval path and identifier will be reported here upon execution [待补充: ethics approval path and identifier]. Any subject may withdraw unconditionally at any time.

### 4.6. On-Device Benchmarking Protocol

On-device performance is measured with a four-part bench protocol (Table T7), executed on the flashed hardware with the serial-command interface. (1) Inference latency: a firmware profiling patch times the pure TFLite Micro `Invoke()` call (excluding preprocessing) over at least 100 consecutive windows, reporting mean ± standard deviation, P95, P99, minimum, and maximum; the first five windows are discarded as cold starts. (2) Power: an inline USB power meter records current in three states (idle with inference paused, AI-active, and BLE-active) and converts to power at the measured rail voltage. (3) INT8 consistency: 200 fixed test beats (100 normal, 100 abnormal, seed 42) are scored by the PC TFLite Runtime and by the on-device TFLite Micro interpreter using the identical INT8 model file, and outputs are compared by mean and max |Δp|, decision agreement at θ = 0.35 and θ = 0.5, and AUC ordering; this test exists because INT8 conversion can degrade small ECG models [#7]. (4) Thermal: the on-chip temperature sensor is logged once per second over 10 min of continuous inference to verify that the 65 °C throttle threshold is not reached in normal operation.

Acceptance criteria are: P95 latency ≤ 120 ms end-to-end (≤ 80 ms pure inference), AI-state power increment ≤ 100 mW over idle, |ΔAUC| ≤ 0.025 between PC and on-device INT8, decision agreement at θ = 0.5 ≥ 94%, and peak temperature ≤ 65 °C with ΔT ≤ 15 °C. Preliminary PC-side checks provide reference points: mean |Δp| ≈ 0.25 between INT8 and FP32 outputs (attributed to double-softmax compression) with |ΔAUC| ≤ 0.025 (project tuning log, Section 13.4); on-device confirmation is pending.

### 5.4. Heart-Rate Validation and Human-Subject Study

**Heart-rate algorithm validation on LUDB.** The energy-envelope QRS detector of Section 3.3 was validated against the annotated Lobachevsky University Database (LUDB, 200 records, 1,831 hand-annotated QRS peaks, lead ii, 500 Hz), rather than only against in-system plausibility. Table T12 reports the deployed energy-envelope detector (v6, 2026-08-16) against the prior energy-envelope release (v5) and the derivative-detector progression: sensitivity of 96.40%, positive predictive value of 78.87%, F1 of 0.868, and a BPM error of 1.46 median / 4.16 mean (TP 1765, FP 473, FN 66 of 1,831 annotated peaks). The re-validation was prompted by the detector change on the device: the derivative and the 5-15 Hz band were replaced by the squared energy envelope and an 8-25 Hz band after real-device debugging (Section 3.3). The first energy-envelope release (v5) inverted the prior limiting metric — sensitivity was no longer the bottleneck — but, with morphological validation disabled (required to stop false rejection of narrow R waves), T-wave and wide-QRS double-counting on 24 of 200 records lowered PPV to 71.03% and inflated the mean BPM error to 10.17 (median 3.15). The deployed v6 re-calibrates the morphology gates in the energy-envelope domain from a peak-level diagnosis of those 24 records (startup blank 260 samples; rise/fall ratio ≤ 40; amplitude ≥ 0.55× previous valid peak for RR < 0.9 s; RR ≥ 0.65× median RR, active from the third beat), recovering PPV to 78.87% and mean BPM error to 4.16 (median 1.46) at a sensitivity cost of 0.54 percentage points (96.94%→96.40%), reported transparently in line with the limitation-aware practice of Schläpfer and Wellens [#48]; for the home-monitoring use case, the BPM estimate remains an averaged quantity.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1. Principal Findings

This work set out to build a complete, low-cost flexible ECG system whose reported numbers survive contact with deployment conditions, and three findings carry the argument. First, patient-level evaluation changes the story a system can honestly tell: under patient isolation, the strongest PTB model reaches AUC 0.8232 in-domain and the deployed MIT-domain model reaches 0.9878 on the non-augmented test set, while the historically leakage-affected PTB evaluation collapses under patient isolation for the same architecture family (quantified in Section 5.6), a correction that reorders what the system claims. Second, the per-class AAMI view exposes ceilings the aggregate hides: ventricular ectopy is well detected (0.952 patient-level), while fusion beats (0.442 patient-level) and supraventricular ectopy at the beat-level reference (0.453) mark the current task ceiling. Third, the training-deployment filter mismatch is measurable and costly (up to ΔAUC −0.105 on PTB), and deployment-chain-matched retraining recovers most of it (PTB D3 0.7697 with the SGD configuration), which to our knowledge is the first quantification of this mismatch for MCU ECG inference.

### 6.2. Per-Class Ceilings Are Task-Composition Limits, Not Model Failure

The SVEB and fusion ceilings deserve precise interpretation. The evidence that they are not model failures is threefold: precision is 1.000 on every abnormal superclass at patient level, so the model almost never confuses one abnormal class with a false firing; ventricular and paced morphologies are learned to recall ≥ 0.95 on both tracks, showing the architecture has adequate capacity for morphologically distinct classes; and the failing classes are exactly those whose defining features (P-wave timing and prematurity for SVEB, intermediate morphology for fusion beats) are attenuated by the single-beat 250-sample window and Z-score normalization. This reading agrees with the wider observation that aggregate ECG classification metrics conceal clinically relevant per-class behavior [#48] and with the per-class difficulty ordering reported in inter-patient studies [#2]. The practical consequence is that richer SVEB detection likely requires rhythm context beyond a 1.0 s window, which is an input-design question rather than an architecture question.

### 6.3. The Cross-Domain Transfer Gap Is Honest and Within the Literature-Normal Range

The PTB transfer results (AUC 0.6174-0.7908 for models that never saw PTB patients) sit well below the in-domain results (0.7319-0.8232), and this paper reports the gap rather than selecting the flattering column. Three compounding difficulties explain it: cross-database transfer with zero target-domain exposure, single-lead input, and a 1.0 s analysis window. The project's literature scan of cross-database ECG classification places typical transfer AUCs in the 0.68-0.85 range, with PTB-like target domains in the lower segment [待复核: numerical range from the project tuning log's literature scan (TUNING_HISTORY §8.1); the underlying sources are not in this paper's verified citation matrix and the range should be cited only if they are admitted at final assembly]. Independent of the exact range, substantial cross-domain degradation is the documented norm rather than the exception in ECG benchmarks [#15], domain generalization on ECG remains an open problem even with adversarial training on 12-lead data [#19], and the same three-database inter-patient protocol of Farag et al. [#2] shows that transfer, not in-domain fitting, is the hard part. The deployment decision that follows is to pair a strong in-domain MI expert (exp6) with a strong arrhythmia expert rather than to claim zero-shot generality the data do not support.

### 6.4. Training-Deployment Consistency Has a Measurable Price

The causal-versus-zero-phase filter mismatch cost up to ΔAUC −0.105 on PTB, which is larger than many architecture-search gains reported in the ECG literature. Deployment-chain-matched retraining recovered most of the cost (PTB D3 0.7697, with the SGD arm +0.035 AUC over AdamW), and on the MIT domain the deployment-chain model even exceeded its training-chain counterpart (0.9122 versus 0.8245). The residual PTB gap of −0.054 is attributed to the firmware's 0.5 Hz high-pass corner, which sits close to diagnostically relevant ST-segment content; lowering it toward 0.05 Hz is the next engineering step, with frequency-response simulation suggesting partial recovery, but that remains an expectation, not a result. Two design lessons follow. First, preprocessing is part of the model: teams deploying behind causal filters should train through an exact replica of the deployed chain, and the INT8 collapse risk documented by Rajotte et al. [#7] extends in spirit to filter-chain mismatches. Second, the linear-phase alternatives that dominate offline ECG filtering [#18] are simply unavailable at run time on this hardware class, which makes the mismatch structural rather than incidental. Beyond the filter chain, the acquisition loop's timing adds a sample-rate-level consistency cost: without a hardware timer the loop ran at roughly 336 Hz instead of 500 Hz, shifting the 50 Hz comb notch off the power-line frequency and dropping the effective recording rate to about 225.7 Hz. Heart-rate RR timing was decoupled from the frame rate to remove the induced BPM overestimate, and the cadence has since been fixed by an esp_timer hardware tick and verified on the real AFE path (496.2 Hz loop; 60 s recording at 245.1 Hz effective). The same design lesson therefore holds at the timing level: any fixed-rate assumption in the deployed chain must be either enforced in hardware or made rate-independent in software.

### 6.5. Limitations

Several limitations bound the claims. (1) The system is single-lead; multi-lead information that supports localization and finer MI characterization [#17, #36] is unavailable by design. (2) The dual-expert OR fusion has been validated PC-side over deployment-chain inputs, but the dual-interpreter firmware configuration is not yet benchmarked on device: its latency, power, and thermal behavior are pending (Section 5.5), and the fused patient-level metrics are pending (Section 5.2). (3) The MIT/INCART test sets contain 6× augmented beats correlated with their originals, so the effective number of independent test samples is overestimated for that corpus; PTB test beats are unaugmented and unaffected. (4) The cross-domain PTB gap (Section 6.3) limits zero-shot deployment to new populations. (5) The deployment-chain correction is incomplete on PTB (−0.054 residual), and a sampling-level gap was identified and closed: the pre-fix acquisition loop ran at roughly 336 Hz instead of the designed 500 Hz (recording ≈225.7 Hz); an esp_timer hardware tick now enforces the cadence, measured at 496.2 Hz on the real AFE path with a 60 s recording at 245.1 Hz effective, leaving a 0.8% residual notch/rate offset to be reported rather than hidden. (6) Electrode characterization, the human-subject study, and the on-device benchmark are protocol-complete but execution-pending, so no electrode, on-human, latency, power, or thermal claim is made; the human-subject study additionally awaits ethics approval. (7) The heart-rate detector's LUDB validation exposes a sensitivity-PPV trade-off: the deployed energy-envelope detector with recalibrated morphology gates reaches 96.40% sensitivity and 78.87% PPV (TP 1765 / FP 473 / FN 66), and the residual 473 false detections keep the mean BPM error at 4.16 (median 1.46), so the BPM feature remains suited to averaged-rate use rather than instantaneous RR measurement. (8) Bootstrap confidence intervals are pending the final evaluation run. (9) The device is a research prototype and is not intended for clinical diagnosis; no clinical claim is made or implied.

### 6.6. Positioning Against Prior Systems

Against the closest high-profile system, the AI-enabled flexible patch of Huang et al. [#5], this work trades integration density for accessibility and evaluation depth: Huang et al. deliver custom patch-level silicon, while this system is assembled from commercial parts at a cost of a few tens of US dollars and adds what [#5] does not report, namely dual-expert arrhythmia-plus-MI fusion, a quantified training-deployment mismatch, and patient-level multi-dataset evaluation with an AAMI class-wise breakdown and leakage audit. Against the MCU-scale works, ArrythML [#1] shares the exact ESP32-S3 but is binary-only, intra-patient, and system-incomplete; Busi et al. [#3] run on specialized RISC-V silicon with intra-patient evaluation and no electrode; Zhou et al. [#6] target a Cortex-M4 with a single dataset; and Panwar et al. [#4] share the AD8232 front end but offload inference to a PC with disposable electrodes. Farag et al. [#2] remain the methodological reference for inter-patient evaluation on the same three databases, executed there on a Raspberry Pi class platform; the present work ports that rigor to bare-metal MCU hardware and extends it with the leakage audit, the per-class AAMI breakdown, and the deployment-chain analysis. The honest summary is that no prior entry, including this one, dominates on every axis; this system is the only one that is simultaneously full-stack, on-device, patient-level, and deployment-chain-matched, and it achieves that at reproducible cost rather than at peak integration.

### 6.7. Reproducibility

Reproducibility is a deliberate contribution. The electrode is a reproduction of a published formulation [#29] with a stepwise preparation protocol; the front end is the widely available AD8232, as in [#4]; the MCU is a commercial ESP32-S3 module; the firmware filter chain has an exact software replica used for deployment-chain-matched training; and the full toolchain from data preparation through training, INT8 export, fusion evaluation, and bench protocols is documented in the project repository, with every headline number traceable to a named evaluation artifact. This stands in contrast to systems whose custom silicon [#5] or specialized processors [#3] place replication out of reach for most laboratories, and it follows the open-benchmark practice that has made PhysioNet-hosted resources so productive for this field [#23].

### 6.8. Future Work

Five directions follow directly. First, execute the three pending protocols (electrode T1-T5, human-subject, on-device bench) and populate the placeholder results of Sections 5.1, 5.4, and 5.5. Second, close the residual deployment-chain gap by lowering the firmware high-pass corner and re-quantifying the mismatch. Third, address the SVEB and fusion ceilings with rhythm-context inputs (multi-beat windows with inter-beat interval features), which the per-class analysis identifies as the binding constraint. Fourth, evaluate the fused dual-expert configuration on device, including its power and thermal budget, and study per-expert threshold pairs rather than a shared θ. Fifth, strengthen cross-domain robustness through domain-adaptation or multi-source training in the spirit of [#19], with the honest cross-domain baseline of this paper as the reference point.

## 7. Conclusions

This paper presented a complete, low-cost flexible ECG system built around a commercial ESP32-S3 dual-core microcontroller, covering the full chain from a self-made PEDOT:PSS:PVA:TA flexible dry electrode through an AD8232 analog front end, dual-core firmware with causal digital filtering and Pan-Tompkins heart-rate detection, on-device INT8 inference, and BLE delivery of waveform plus interpretation to a Flutter application. Four contributions define the work. First, a dual-expert on-device AI fuses an arrhythmia beat-classification expert and a myocardial infarction detection expert through a logical OR rule, so a single-lead wearable flags either abnormality class in real time; to our knowledge, this arrhythmia-plus-MI dual-expert fusion has not been reported on MCU-class hardware. Second, deployment-chain-matched training quantifies the cost of training on zero-phase preprocessed data while deploying behind causal filters (up to ΔAUC −0.105 on PTB) and recovers most of it (PTB deployment-chain AUC 0.7697). Third, evaluation rigor: patient-level 60/20/20 splits across MIT-BIH, INCART, and PTB with an explicit leakage audit yield honest headline numbers (PTB in-domain AUC 0.8232, MIT-domain AUC 0.8874 for the patient-clean arms; MIT 0.9740 for the deployed P2A model), and the AAMI class-wise breakdown exposes per-class ceilings (fusion 0.442 patient-level; SVEB 0.453 beat-level reference) that aggregate metrics hide. Fourth, a complete and reproducible stack: every part is commercial off-the-shelf or a reproduction of a published formulation, and every headline number traces to a named evaluation artifact.

The scope is stated plainly. The system is a research prototype for methodology and engineering evaluation; it is not a medical device and is not intended for clinical diagnosis. Electrode characterization, the human-subject study, and on-device benchmarking are protocol-complete with measurements pending, the dual-interpreter firmware configuration awaits on-device benchmarking, the cross-domain transfer gap and the per-class ceilings are reported as limitations rather than decorated as strengths, and the historical leakage-affected metrics appear only as a methodological contrast. Within that scope, the system demonstrates that a few-tens-of-dollars platform can carry an evaluation protocol whose numbers are built to survive deployment.
